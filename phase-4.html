Phase 4 · Training → Inference trade-offs

Choices made once, paid for forever

Every decision during pretraining echoes through every inference request for the lifetime of the model. This phase teaches you to make those choices on purpose.

Scope how pretraining choices affect serving cost and quality
Time · LOE 1 week · 6–10 hours
Deliverable compare 2–3 model / config choices and justify the trade-offs

Trade-offs in pictures

Bigger isn’t always better. The right model is the smallest one that hits your quality bar at your serving cost.

Quality vs cost

Bigger model = more quality, more cost per token. The curve is sub-linear: doubling model size rarely doubles quality. Find the inflection point for your task.

Distillation

Train a small “student” to match a large “teacher”’s outputs (logits or behavior). Result: serve a 7B that often beats a vanilla 7B because it learned from a 70B’s judgments.

Mixture of Experts

N expert sub-models, but a router activates only top-K per token. More total parameters (more capacity) at the same active compute cost. Used in Mixtral, GPT-4-class systems.

Quantization-aware training

Simulate quantization during training so the model learns to be robust to it. Costs more training; recovers nearly all of the quality you’d lose to post-training quantization.

Context length isn’t free

Training on 32k context costs more than 2k. Serving 128k costs much more — KV cache scales linearly with context, attention scales quadratically. Pick the shortest context that meets your needs.

Right-sizing by use case

A classification model doesn’t need 70B. A reasoning agent might. Map your task to the smallest model that hits the quality bar; revisit when models or hardware improve.

The questions you should be able to answer

  • What does the next 100× in tokens-per-second buy you (latency, cost, scale)?
  • If you halve the model size, how much quality do you actually lose on your benchmark?
  • If you add 4× the context length, what breaks first — training time, GPU memory at serve, or attention cost?
  • Distill or quantize: which buys you more, and at what training cost?
  • How does MoE change your story (training hardware, inference hardware, scheduler complexity)?

Hands-on curriculum

Checkpoint 1 · Quality vs cost study

Pick a benchmark. Run 7B / 13B / 70B (or three open models you have access to).

  • Exercise: report quality and cost-per-million-tokens for each.
  • Exercise: pick the right model for a hypothetical use case.
  • Pass when: you can defend the choice with numbers, not vibes.

Checkpoint 2 · Distillation

Use a strong teacher to fine-tune a smaller student on your task.

  • Exercise: collect teacher outputs on a corpus.
  • Exercise: fine-tune student on teacher outputs.
  • Pass when: distilled student beats vanilla student of the same size.

Checkpoint 3 · Quantization comparison

Take one model, three precisions: fp16, int8, int4.

  • Exercise: benchmark quality (your eval) and cost (tokens/sec/dollar).
  • Exercise: identify the precision wall for your use case.
  • Pass when: you can recommend a default precision and justify it.

Checkpoint 4 · Final report

Produce a one-pager comparing 2–3 model / config choices for a real internal use case.

  • Exercise: tabulate quality, latency, cost, ops complexity.
  • Exercise: make a recommendation with caveats.
  • Pass when: a non-ML engineer can read your report and know what to do.
